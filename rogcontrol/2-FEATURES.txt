ROG CONTROL — WHAT IT DOES
==========================

A G-Helper-style control panel for ASUS ROG laptops on Linux, without needing
asusctl. One window, a tray icon, and background services that keep your
settings in force.


PROFILES

Four to start with — Quiet, Balanced Power, Balanced Performance, Performance
— and you can add your own.

A profile holds everything at once: CPU power limits, undervolt, turbo on or
off, a maximum clock, an energy preference, GPU limits, and a fan curve for
each of the three fans. Switching profile applies all of it and moves the
system power mode to match, so the OS and the app never disagree.

The two Balanced profiles have identical CPU and GPU limits and differ in how
hard the CPU chases clocks — one leans to battery life, the other to
response. Balanced Performance also runs its fans a little higher than
Balanced Power, since it is the one meant for when you notice the fans less
than the response.

New and Delete live in the header menu, one profile at a time. Export writes
EVERYTHING — every profile, which one is current, the charge limit, keyboard
settings, auto-switch targets, fan RPM calibration — to one JSON file, so it
doubles as a full backup. Import reads it back: point it at a backup and it
asks to confirm before replacing everything with what is in the file: this
is a restore, not a merge, and there is no undo. Point it at an older
single-profile export instead and it merges that one profile in safely, same
as it always has, renaming it rather than overwriting anything with the same
name.


FANS

An eight-point curve per fan, dragged on a graph showing real RPM rather than
percentages. Eight points is exactly what the hardware stores, so what you
draw is what runs — nothing is interpolated behind your back. Arrow keys nudge
a selected point for fine adjustment.

"Calibrate fan RPM" measures how your own fans respond, so the graph tells the
truth on your machine rather than on the developer's.

Curves are re-asserted in the background, because the embedded controller
throws them away every time the power mode changes.


CPU

On AMD: STAPM, fast and slow power limits, temperature target, Curve
Optimizer undervolt, turbo boost, and a maximum and minimum core clock.

On Intel: PL1 (sustained) and PL2 (short-burst) power limits, through the
firmware's own asus-wmi control where the model exposes it, or RAPL as a
fallback -- turbo boost, the energy preference and the clock ceiling and
floor work the same as on AMD. No undervolt and no temperature target;
neither has an Intel equivalent on this platform. A model with neither the
firmware control nor a writable RAPL limit shows a notice instead of a
control that could only fail -- see docs/INTEL-SUPPORT-PLAN.txt. The System
page's "Save hardware report" button, or `rogcontrol --hardware-report`, is
how to find out which this machine is.

Nothing is written until you press Apply. Moving a control changes what the
page is asking for and says so in a banner at the top; the chip is untouched
until you decide. Apply writes the whole page in the one order that works —
the limits, then boost, then the energy preference, then the clock ceiling
and last the clock floor, because the boost switch resets both and the
ceiling write can pull the floor down with it — and saves it to the
active profile only once the hardware has taken it. Leaving the page without
applying discards the change; it is never applied behind your back.

The clock setting is a ceiling, not a fixed speed — cores still drop to idle
underneath it. Measured with a 3.0 GHz cap: a loaded core sat at 2974 MHz
while idle cores sat at 1492 MHz.

Turning turbo boost off greys the maximum clock out, and the ceiling is not
written while it is off: boost off already pins every core at its base clock,
and the boost write itself puts every policy's maximum back to the hardware's
own on its way past. The value is kept, and comes back the moment boost does.
The minimum clock is unaffected and is still applied.

The undervolt carries a warning for a reason. Too negative freezes the machine
under load; this laptop locked solid at −20. Move two or three counts at a
time and test.


GPU

Power limit, core and memory clock offsets, a clock ceiling, NVIDIA Dynamic
Boost and temperature target. Ranges are read from your card, not hardcoded.

Applied on the same terms as the CPU page: an Apply button, a banner naming
what is not on the card yet, and a profile that is only written once the card
has accepted the setting.

The graphics mode: what the display is plugged into now, and a picker to
switch between Integrated, Hybrid and AsusMuxDgpu. Integrated and Hybrid swap
by restarting the display stack; switching into or out of AsusMuxDgpu moves
the hardware MUX, which only the firmware can do, so that one offers to
reboot for you. Whatever supergfxd answers is repeated on the page word for
word, so a refusal says which of several reasons applied.

Live CPU and GPU temperatures and the speed of the fan cooling each are shown
on their own pages, refreshed every two seconds. The GPU reads "Idle" while
the card is asleep — and stays asleep, because the page checks its power
state in sysfs rather than waking it with nvidia-smi to ask.


KEYBOARD

Brightness, and eleven lighting modes: Static, Breathing, Pulse, Colour Cycle,
Rainbow, Gradient Static, GPU Temp Colour, CPU Temp Colour, Battery Level,
Ambient, and Profile Colour.

Ambient follows what is on screen: the display is split into four vertical
bands, each lighting the zone beneath it. It uses the desktop's screen-sharing
portal, so permission is asked once and remembered.

Profile Colour gives each power profile its own colour, set on the Keyboard
page, and repaints the keys whenever the profile changes -- from the window,
the tray, the shortcut key, the OS power menu, or the AC/battery auto-switch.
It works with the app closed.

Charger flash is off until you switch it on: the keys blink one colour twice
when the charger is plugged in or unplugged and then go straight back to the
effect they were on. It runs in the background service, so it works with the
window closed, and it lands about a second after the plug moves. GPU Temp,
CPU Temp and Battery Level restore to a fresh sensor reading rather than a
stale one. If the keyboard has gone idle enough that its own firmware cut
backlight power, the flash wakes it first and puts the brightness back to
exactly what it was after. Ambient is left alone -- its colour is not in
the config at all, so there would be nothing to put back afterwards.

Modes your hardware cannot perform are not offered rather than failing
silently.


BATTERY

A charge limit, and automatic profile switching on plug and unplug — pick a
profile for mains and one for battery. That runs in the background service, so
it works whether or not the window is open.


SYSTEM

Whether supergfxd — the daemon the graphics-mode picker needs — is installed
and answering. Some distributions ship the supergfxctl package with its
service switched off, which looks from the GPU page like a broken or missing
feature; when that is what has happened, this row says so and offers a button
to enable and start it. There is no button to turn it off again: without it
the graphics mode can be neither read nor changed.

Whether asusctl's daemon, asusd, is installed and running. It matters because
it drives the same hardware as this app — the same platform knobs, fan curves
and keyboard lighting — so with both running they fight over the fans and the
lights. There are buttons to stop and disable it and to put it back. There is
no uninstall button: removing a package is a transaction you should see, so
the exact command for your distro is shown for you to run yourself.

A switch for the boot sound — the chime the firmware plays through the
speakers at power-on. It is a setting of the machine rather than of a profile,
so it does not change when you switch one, and it is remembered so the
boot-apply service can put it back if a BIOS update resets the firmware.

A switch for panel overdrive, on machines whose firmware offers it. It pushes
each pixel transition past its target so the screen changes state faster and
fast motion smears less. It is a trade rather than a free improvement — too
much of it shows as a pale ghost leading a moving edge — so it is a switch
you own. Like the boot sound it belongs to the machine and not to a profile,
and it is remembered and re-asserted at login.

ALSO

  * Live RAM and VRAM use on the overview page, beside the temperatures
  * A tray icon that shows and switches the active profile
  * Keyboard shortcuts for cycling profiles and lighting modes
  * A page whose live readings stop coming back says so instead of showing
    dashes — dashes mean "this machine has no such sensor", and a sampler
    that has broken is a different thing that used to look identical
  * Every keyboard shortcut that fails tells you why, rather than only that
    it failed
  * Settings survive suspend, reboot and firmware resets — a background
    service re-asserts them
  * Everything you configure is in one file, ~/.config/rogcontrol.json
  * Anything that goes wrong with no window open is written to
    ~/.local/share/rogcontrol/rogcontrol.log


WHAT WE LEARNED MEASURING THIS LAPTOP
-------------------------------------

Some of this took days to pin down and is worth knowing if you are chasing the
same things.

The fans "ignoring" your curve
    The embedded controller reads a CPU temperature Linux never exposes. It
    takes short excursions into the 85-90 °C band several times a minute while
    k10temp reports 60 °C, and it applies YOUR curve to that hidden number. So
    the fans are not disobeying the curve, they are obeying it at a temperature
    you cannot see. The custom curve stays enabled throughout — verified over
    179 consecutive samples.

    Nothing on the software side changes this: not a clock cap, turbo off,
    undervolt, power limits, energy preference, the GPU, or how often anything
    is polled. All measured, all ruled out.

    What does work is the curve's shape. Hold one value across the whole band
    those hidden readings reach (roughly 50-90 °C) and put the real ramp above
    it. The excursion then has nothing to climb to. Measured: fan swing dropped
    from 600 rpm to 100-300 rpm, with nothing above 2400 rpm.

    All four stock profiles ship with that shape — flat from 50 °C to 86 °C,
    the ramp above 90 °C, scaled per profile — so a fresh install is quiet
    without having to find this out first.

There is a minimum fan speed, and it is not zero
    5 %, 10 % and 15 % all produce 2100-2600 rpm on this machine — that is the
    slowest these fans will spin. Below that they stop completely (0 % gives
    0 rpm). "Quiet but spinning" under about 2100 rpm is not a setting, it is
    not a capability.

Fan curve writes need spacing
    The controller silently drops curve writes fired less than about eight
    seconds apart across channels. That is why applying a curve takes roughly
    sixteen seconds and cannot be hurried.

Turbo boost and the fans
    A boost spike hits 85-90 °C for a few milliseconds even at idle, which is
    enough for the controller to react. Turning boost off removes those spikes,
    at the cost of peak single-thread speed.

Energy preference and the performance governor
    Setting the OS power mode to Performance switches the CPU governor to
    "performance", and the kernel then ignores the energy preference entirely.
    That is expected, not a failure — the governor already pins maximum
    performance.

Screen capture for Ambient
    On Wayland the desktop portal is the only route. Screenshots were measured
    at 1.5-5 seconds each and a 4 MB file per capture, so the mode streams
    instead, paced to two frames a second.
